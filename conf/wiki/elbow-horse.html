Elbow horse

The elbow horse (simplified Chinese: 卧槽马; traditional Chinese: 臥槽馬; pinyin: wò cáo mǎ) is an attacking horse placement in xiangqi. In its modern form, the horse stands just outside the opposing palace, one point in front of either original elephant position. It can check the general on the central point of the back rank and cooperate with other pieces to restrict escape, win material, or deliver checkmate. Jim Png Hau Cheng describes it as one of xiangqi’s most important attacking positions.[1]

An older use of the name places the horse one file farther toward the edge of the board, in front of an original horse position. This usage appears in the opening literature, including Secrets Inside the Orange (橘中秘, 1632). This older location is the position of the golden hooked horse (金钩马).[2]

The motif appears in classical compositions, instructional literature, and tournament play. It also occurs in xiangqi proverbs and personal recollections, and has been used figuratively in sports writing and television drama to describe a threatening or concealed force.

Terminology and history

Chinese and English names

The Chinese name combines wò (卧, “lie down” or “recline”), cáo (槽, “trough”), and mǎ (马, “horse”).[3] An essay published by the Shenzhen Qiguo Xiangqi Museum connects the name with the original horse station, known as the “trough position” (槽位). In this explanation, the older elbow horse stands beside the opponent’s trough.[4]

An alternative explanation in the essay “Language knowledge in xiangqi” derives the expression from wō cháo (窝巢, “nest”), an image for the general’s home. It proposes that the term arose through wordplay involving the sounds and order of these characters.[5]

The modern position lies on the third or seventh file, known as the “elbow files”. Png attributes this designation to the Asian Xiangqi Federation.[2]

In his Lexicon of Xiangqi/Chinese Chess Terms in English, Png uses “elbow horse” and records Felix Tan’s proposed alternative, “moat horse”. Tan’s analogy refers to the moat or other defenses around a palace: an enemy able to maneuver cavalry and build stables outside it poses a serious threat.[2] “Elbow” is not a literal translation of the Chinese name.[3]

Classical usage

Png distinguishes the modern elbow horse on the third or seventh file from an older usage on the second or eighth file of the same rank. The latter corresponds to the golden hooked horse (金钩马), one file farther from the palace.[2][6]

Elegant Pastime (适情雅趣), a collection associated with 1570, contains compositions using the modern elbow-horse position. Xu Zhi (徐芝) is credited as its compiler and Chen Xueli (陈学礼) as its editor. In “Lowering the Reins to Rescue the Lord” and “Building a Bridge upon Meeting Water”, the horse’s early check initiates a longer combination in which it later takes on a different role.[7][8]

Zhu Jinzhen’s (朱晋桢) Secrets Inside the Orange preserves the older meaning in a line headed “Same-direction cannons, upright chariot, elbow horse” (顺炮直车卧槽马). Black’s horse advances to the point in front of Red’s original horse position, then withdraws to a palace corner with check. The horse occupies a different file from the modern elbow horse.[9]

In Secrets Inside the Orange, 8... h3+2 establishes the older elbow-horse position. The later 10... h2-4 gives check from a palace corner.[9]

The older placement also appears in a free-hand-cannon line (信手炮) attributed to Unbeatable Upon Leaving the Cave (自出洞来无敌手). Red’s horse reaches the point in front of Black’s original horse position, later gives check from a palace corner, and finally serves as a cannon screen.[10]

Language, literature and cultural reception

Sayings and literary allusions

The saying “连车重炮卧槽马，城里老将挨死打” (“Linked chariots, stacked cannons, an elbow horse—the general in the city takes a mortal beating”) groups the elbow horse with other coordinated attacking formations.[11]

Tournament commentary also draws on literary images of horses. A 2014 report of Zhao Xinxin’s (赵鑫鑫) game against Zheng Weitong (郑惟桐) describes the decisive horse maneuver with “the horse raced as swiftly as Dilu” (马作的卢飞快). The line comes from Xin Qiji’s (辛弃疾) “Breaking the Battle Formation: A Heroic Lyric Sent to Chen Tongfu” (破阵子·为陈同甫赋壮词以寄之), transferring an image of mounted battle to the chessboard.[12][13]

Classical problem titles give the horse’s maneuvers a narrative form. In later annotations to Elegant Pastime, “Lowering the Reins to Rescue the Lord” takes its meaning from a horse’s return to protect its own general; in “Building a Bridge upon Meeting Water”, the horse forms the “bridge” by becoming a cannon screen.[8]

Recollections of play

Biographical accounts place the term among the named tactics learned in childhood. A profile of Zhao Guorong (赵国荣) recalls his learning the elbow horse before school, while a profile of Zang Ruyi (臧如意) describes his early exposure to tactical vocabulary through Beijing teahouse play.[14][15]

In a 2023 Yangcheng Evening News essay, Chen Jicheng (陈积成) recalls learning the motif from a book by Yang Guanlin (杨官璘) and later cycling across the city to watch the Five Rams Cup.[16]

A 2012 United Daily News newsletter essay by the writer Five Trees (五木) recounts a game against a blind opponent using a tactile board. The narrator expects an H8+7 elbow-horse attack to succeed, but the opponent captures the horse with a retreating cannon. The missed defense provides the story’s reversal.[17]

Figurative uses

A 2006 Shanghai Youth Daily football article compares an attacking midfield arrangement to an elbow horse, using the image of a piece stationed close to the opponent’s vulnerable center.[18] In China Central Television’s synopsis of episode 15 of Jinggangshan (井冈山), Zeng Shi’e (曾士峨) is described as an elbow horse: a concealed asset available to Mao Zedong (毛泽东).[19]

Tactical role

Attack and coordination

Png treats the elbow horse as a fundamental attacking position, describing elbow-horse checkmate as a technique in which the horse restricts the general while another piece delivers mate.[2] A modern elbow horse stands on the second rank from the opponent’s back edge, immediately outside the palace. With its inward leg unobstructed, it attacks the central points on the palace’s front and back ranks, but not the central point on the middle rank. A mating attack therefore requires other pieces to close the general’s remaining escape routes.[20][21]

A chariot can cut off the general’s escape along the middle rank, while the horse checks from the side. The horse may also give check and simultaneously attack a chariot on an outside file.[4] In horse-and-cannon combinations, it can force the general onto an exposed line or later become a cannon screen.[8][10] An elbow horse can also cooperate with a second horse that controls an escape route, as in the pattern “two horses drinking at the spring” (双马饮泉).[22]

The horse need not remain on the elbow-horse point throughout an attack. Moving between attacking positions can uncover checks by another piece and win material.[1]

Figure 15 in Yang Guanlin’s New Compilation from the Chess World (弈林新编). The quiet move 2. H4+2 prepares the elbow horse; 5. H2+3 later delivers mate with the chariot’s support.[21]

Elbow-horse attacks can also win material. In the 2010 national league, Li Hongjia (李鸿嘉) used a coordinated attack against Chen Zhuo (陈卓) to win a cannon.[23] In a 2012 game against Wang Yang (汪洋), Nie Tiewen (聂铁文) offered a chariot against an advisor; accepting it would have allowed an elbow-horse mate. Wang resigned.[24]

Defense

Defenses include preventing the horse’s entry, blocking its checking leg, capturing or exchanging it, moving the general, and removing its supporting pieces. Wu Guilin’s (吴贵临) Xiangqi Art of War (象棋兵法) compares interpositions and counterattacks against the motif.[25]

The leg used to enter the elbow-horse position differs from the leg used to check once there. An advisor on a palace corner can obstruct an approach from that direction, but it cannot occupy the point directly between an established elbow horse and the middle rank of the palace. A chariot or cannon can block that checking leg, although an interposition may expose the defender to another threat.[25]

The advanced horse can itself become a target. Xu Yinchuan (许银川) trapped Hong Zhi’s (洪智) horse in the January 2010 year-end final.[26] In the 2013 Covered Bridge Cup, Tang Dan (唐丹) exchanged a cannon for Zhang Guofeng’s (张国凤) attacking horse.[27] A report of Zheng Weitong’s game against Wang Tianyi (王天一) in the 2018 National Cup criticized Zheng’s rapid horse advance because his other pieces had not developed enough to support it.[28]

Classical compositions and endings

Lowering the Reins to Rescue the Lord

Problem 24 of Elegant Pastime, “Lowering the Reins to Rescue the Lord” (垂缰救主), begins with the elbow-horse check 1. H2+3, driving Black’s general off the central file. The combination develops through two horses: one removes a central pawn, while the other eventually captures an advisor and returns to shield Red’s general. This releases the chariot to give check along the back rank.[8]

23. H4-6 shields Red’s general. After Black’s reply, 24. R7+8 gives check with the released chariot.[8]

Building a Bridge upon Meeting Water

In problem 45, “Building a Bridge upon Meeting Water” (遇水叠桥), 2. H2+3 establishes an elbow-horse check early in the attack. Horse-and-cannon cooperation wins a chariot, and a later chariot sacrifice redirects a defender. The horse then circles back to become the screen for the final cannon check. The mate is completed by 15. H2-4.[8]

The horse’s final position supplies the cannon screen described as a “bridge” in the annotation.[8]

Another problem, “Lone Deer Calling in the Marsh” (独鹿鸣泽), contrasts an elbow-horse approach with a palace-corner sacrifice. Its solution begins 1. H8+6, after which Black’s advisor captures the horse. Chariot and cannon attacks then exploit the changed arrangement.[8][4]

Horse-and-pawn ending

Ending 28 in New Compilation from the Chess World uses the elbow horse to restrict the elephants while Black’s general stands on the central point of the palace’s middle rank, outside the horse’s attack. The annotation describes waiting for the elephants’ movement to become obstructed, then withdrawing the horse so that the pawn can advance. The line concludes with a winning evaluation for Red.[21]

7. H8+7 establishes the elbow horse without check. The horse later withdraws, and the line ends with 12. P7+1.[21]

Tournament examples

The 2019 national final between Wang Yang, playing Red, and Wang Tianyi, playing Black, includes elbow-horse incursions by both sides. Red gives check with 13. H6+7, but after a delay in bringing up support, Black attacks the horse with 14... c8-5 and it withdraws. Black later establishes an elbow horse with 33... h5+7; Red returns to the post with 43. H6+7. Wang Tianyi won the game.[29]

Wang Yang–Wang Tianyi, 2019 national final. Black’s 14... c8-5 attacks the advanced horse.[29]

The motif can influence play through an unexecuted threat. In the 2019 Gaogang Cup final, Meng Chen (孟辰) developed his other horse instead of immediately giving the expected elbow-horse check against Wang Tianyi. A cannon committed to defense became vulnerable to a chariot maneuver, and Wang resigned with material still equal.[30] Tang Zhuoguang’s (汤卓光) analysis of the drawn 2023 Five Rams Cup final between Hong Zhi and Zheng Weitong identifies an alternative cannon move that would have allowed an elbow-horse mating attack; the continuation was avoided in the game.[31]

Material gained through the motif does not always determine the result. Tang Dan won material with horse-and-cannon threats against Wang Yang in the 2011 Dadi Cup but lost on time. In the veterans’ group of the 2026 Five Rams Cup, Yu Youhua (于幼华) used the elbow horse to win a major piece against Liu Dahua (柳大华), then blundered and lost.[32][33]

Teaching

The Chinese Xiangqi Association includes recognition of the elbow horse in its grade-15 assessment requirements.[34] It also appears in school curricula: Taipei Xinhe Elementary School’s 2024 club plan schedules it in week 5, and Taipei Zhishan Elementary School’s spring 2025 plan pairs it with the palace-corner horse in week 11.[35][36]

Instructional books treat the motif as a subject in its own right. Yang Guanlin includes it in both mating combinations and practical endings in New Compilation from the Chess World. Jin Qichang (金启昌) and others devoted 100 Elbow-Horse Mating Combinations (卧槽马杀100着, 2000) to it.[21][37]
